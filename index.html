I'm a computational cancer genomicist and Postdoctoral Scholar in Ludmil Alexandrov's lab at UC San Diego. I work at the intersection of cancer genomics, mutational signatures and the microbiome, studying how microbes and other exposures leave their marks on human cancer genomes, with a focus on early-onset colorectal cancer.

Where I started

I came to cancer research through computer science. I earned my BA magna cum laude in Computer Science and Biology at Mount Holyoke College, built a genotyping data application across 32 clinical trials at Novartis, and spent two years at the Massachusetts General Hospital Cancer Center developing pipelines that linked therapy-induced APOBEC3A mutagenesis to drug resistance in lung cancer. During my PhD in Bioinformatics and Systems Biology at UC San Diego, I developed HRProfiler, a patented machine-learning tool that detects homologous recombination deficiency, a marker of response to platinum and PARP-inhibitor therapy, and contributed to DeepHRD, the SigProfiler tools and copy-number signatures.

What I do now

Through the Cancer Grand Challenges team Mutographs, working with the International Agency for Research on Cancer and the Wellcome Sanger Institute, I led the microbiome analyses of 981 colorectal cancer genomes from 11 countries. They revealed colibactin-associated mutations enriched in younger patients, but also that detecting the colibactin-producing pks bacteria at diagnosis does not reliably match those mutations. That gap now drives my work. As a postdoc I build pksProfiler and CMPipeline to detect genotoxic bacteria in tumour genomes and metagenomes, and knowledge graphs, augmented by large language models, that connect microbial and host data with published evidence.

Where I'm going

When do microbes damage our DNA, and when do they help damaged cells grow?

Colorectal cancer is rising fastest in younger generations. Colibactin's mutational signature can be acquired in the first decade of life, yet pks+ bacteria are also common in people who never develop cancer, and other microbes, such as Fusobacterium nucleatum, may instead help already-mutated cells expand.

My research asks when, and under which host and microbial conditions, microbial exposures cause lasting mutations or promote the progression of initiated cells, and whether those conditions can be changed to prevent colorectal cancer.

  • 01
    Mapping microbial exposure across the life course. Benchmarked tools that detect pks+ bacteria, applied from infancy through adulthood to cancer, to learn when carriage begins, how long it lasts, and which microbial communities come first.
  • 02
    AI-augmented knowledge graphs. Combining patient-level microbial and host data with published biology to find the host–microbe interactions behind mutagenesis and the selection of mutant cells, then testing them with experimental collaborators.
  • 03
    Microbiome-informed prevention. Testing whether changing microbial communities can reduce genotoxic exposure early in life, or limit the expansion of cells that already carry mutations.

My work has appeared in Nature, Nature Genetics, Cancer Cell, Cancer Research and the Journal of Clinical Oncology. I'm always interested in discussing research collaborations. The best way to reach me is by emailing amabbasi [at] ucsd [dot] edu.
